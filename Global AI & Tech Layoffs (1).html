Data Quality Observations

- Missing values: 461 missing values (1,389 of 1,850 rows filled in, ~25%). This seems to be consistent with company status — private companies don't have a public stock price, so this is missing not at random and should be flagged and not imputed.
- Overlapping categories: The industry column contains overlapping labels, such as "E-Commerce" vs. "E-Commerce / Cloud", "Social Media" vs. "Social Media / AI", "Electric Vehicles" vs. "Electric Vehicles / AI"). These are the same sector split as before, just with the inclusion of an AI-related sub-tag; they should be grouped together cleanly.
- The number of numbers laid off is heavily skewed to the right, with the median number being lower than the mean number (658 vs 4,453), and the maximum number laid off being two orders of magnitude higher than the median number (205,254 vs 658). Medians and log scales are better than means at summarizing this field because a few large cutbacks are responsible for a large portion of the distribution.
- The number of employees in the company, represented by company_size_est, spans from 80 to 1,540,000, which is considered a suspicious outlier size. A total of 1.54 million employees is far more than any real tech company – including Amazon's global workforce, which includes only warehouse employees, and is under 1.6M. This may be a data entry error or an outlier, so it should be investigated before distorting any size determinations.
- For firms for which information is available, the stock reaction in the 1-week period around the announcement of a layoff is slightly negative (mean ≈ -2.0% and median ≈ -2.0%), indicating that markets on average interpret layoffs as a slight negative signal (as opposed to a confidence boost), although the spread of reactions (-15.9% to +10.3%) indicates that reactions can be widely different depending on the circumstances.

The core structure of the data is clean: No duplicate rows, no missing values in categorical or date fields.

Preprocessing Summary

- Dates: date_announced was converted to datetime and confirmed that it matches the year column.
- Based on initial exploration, the value of company_size_est = 1,540,000 appeared to be a data entry mistake and has been corrected. This number, on inspection, goes to Amazon (Public), which has a workforce of about this many people worldwide, primarily in the warehouses and logistics. This is not a data quality problem — there was no need to cap, and the ‘cap’ applied higher had no impact as it is the legitimate maximum. Always check the outliers before assuming they are errors!
- Eliminated 3 industry variants which contain AI tags (e.g. Social Media / AI became Social Media) and correctly transferred the associated rows to the correct industry (e.g. 106 rows were moved to Social Media and 137 were moved to Social Media / AI).
- stock_price_change_1wk_pct is missing in 100% of Private companies, and 0% of Public companies. This means that the missingness is not random, but has been explained by the company status, and that's why it was not imputed, but rather used as a boolean to ensure analytical integrity.
- To aid the upcoming insight visualizations, two new derived fields were added: layoff_cause_type (AI-Driven vs. Traditional, 380 vs. 1,470) and size_bucket (company size tier).
 

Insight 1: AI-Driven Layoffs Are Steadily Displacing Traditional Layoff Causes

Why this matters for the business: To determine if layoffs are the result of short-term economic cycles (reversible) or a new role shift towards AI driven automation (irreversible), workforce planning teams need to understand what is driving layoffs. The more AI-related layoffs are increasing, the more it's a sign that HR and leadership should invest in reskilling initiatives and reassess long-term workforce planning, instead of simply waiting out a bad year.

Insight 3: Tech Layoffs Occur in Sharp Waves Tied to Specific Periods, Not a Steady Trickle

Why this matters for the business: If the number of people let go were more or less the same each month, the business would be easy to plan. However, if they tend to come in a spike form, then HR and finance should have contingency plans in place for rapid hiring freezes and severance costs in certain windows and be able to predict when that next window is going to occur based on historical wave timing rather than being caught by surprise.

Final Discussion & Conclusion

Strengths of This Analysis

- Used multiple analytical perspectives (time, market, geography, company size, industry, severance policy) to create a comprehensive picture and not a single-minded conclusion.
- Data quality problems were not taken for granted — the company size "outlier" (Amazon) was analyzed and turned out to be a real outlier, the missing stock data was analyzed, and it was confirmed that it was a structural problem, not a blind imputation. This sort of verification avoids the possibility of misleading inferences.
- This is another good reminder that not all hypotheses are supported by data; the assumption that public companies provide better severance, for example, was not well supported, which is a good and honest business finding.

Limitations

- The data applies to layoffs — not headcount change in general; it may not reflect the rehiring of former employees, so it provides no information on net change of workforce.
- Data for 2026 is not complete (only available to February), and any 2026 figure should be interpreted as a provisional figure rather than for comparison with 2025.
- primary_reason is a categorical word for each event; in reality, many factors can be at play during a real layoff, so this is a way to make fewer reasons look easier.
- Country-level analysis will be based on the HQ country, which may not be the country of the layoffs, as in an example, a US-HQ company is laying off an overseas office.

Key Insights & Implications for the Business

1. The percentage of layoffs driven by AI has increased from ~12% to ~38% of events since 2024, a structural change, not a mere blip.
2. Unlike the "efficiency signal" premise, markets seem to respond a bit more negatively to AI-fueled job cuts.
3. Job losses hit in sharp multi-year waves (2022-23 pandemic correction, recurring waves since).
4. There are patterns of layoffs in September/October and February/March.
5. Exposure to AI is widespread – not limited to AI native verticals; E-Commerce/Gaming, Data/AI and Biotech/AI are the most exposed verticals.
6. Startups suffer cutbacks at a higher percentage when they cut back on employees.
7. The majority of volume is from the U.S., India and China (78% of the world's volume).
There appears to be no real difference in 8. Severance generosity by public/private status.

Data-Driven Recommendations

- Re-skilling investments should be made for AI-exposed jobs in 2024+ as there is no risk of the job being eliminated.
- Communications strategy: Avoid the "AI efficiency" framing as it can have a negative impact on investor sentiment; treat layoffs as you would any other announcement.
- Seasonal readiness: Build up extra HR/legal capacity in advance of each season (Sep-Oct and Feb-March).
- Geographic focus: Focus on the labor law and severance policy in the United States, India and China, as this accounts for the majority of the activity.
- Investors and partners considering a small company($<500 employee) should anticipate that, if layoffs, should occur, they will be more significant than in larger companies.
